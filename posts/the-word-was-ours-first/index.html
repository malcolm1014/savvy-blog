The Word Was Ours First

A field guide to the hacker community for the person walking into their first con — where the word came from, the people who carry it, and the one line you never cross.

By Malcolm R. Smith — Savvy Security LLC · 1 October 2026

I · The Word II · The Ethic III · The Tribe IV · The Name V · Better Angels VI · The Underbelly VII · The Ledger The Film Sources

Before you say the word out loud

I have answered to “hacker” for the better part of thirty years. In that time I have watched the word get me thrown out of a conversation and, ten minutes later, get me invited into a room nobody else in the building knew existed. It is the most misunderstood job title in the world, and it is mine.

If you are new — if you are reading this before your first DEF CON, your first local 2600 meeting, your first BSides — this is the orientation I wish someone had handed me. It is the history of a word that started as a compliment at MIT, the people who still carry it, the conventions where you will meet them, the genuinely good work the community does, the genuinely evil people who try to hide inside it, and the single decision that separates a thirty-year career from a federal indictment.

Everything here is sourced. I quote the founders in their own words wherever I can, and I cite the scholars who have studied this culture for forty years. Read it slowly. Then go introduce yourself.

Part I — The Word

It was a compliment before it was an accusation

The word did not come from a prison, a basement, or a newspaper. It came from a model-railroad clubroom at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. In the first edition of the Tech Model Railroad Club dictionary, compiled in 1959, a “hack” was defined as a project undertaken not for its result but for the sheer joy of the doing — and a “hacker” was the person who did it. The club’s Signals and Power Committee — the members who ran the relays and switches under the layout — used “hack” for a clever improvement to the system. When those same students got their hands on the institute’s early computers, the word walked over with them.

So the original hacker was not a criminal. The original hacker was the kid who stayed up all night because the machine was more interesting than sleep, who improved a thing nobody asked him to improve, who understood a system deeply enough to make it do something its designers never imagined. That is the definition I still use. That is the definition the community still defends.

The phone came before the computer

Before anyone was breaking into networks, a loose fraternity of the curious was exploring the largest machine on earth: the telephone system. The phone phreaks discovered that a 2,600-hertz tone — famously reproducible with a toy whistle that came free in a box of Cap’n Crunch cereal — could seize a long-distance trunk line and hand the caller the keys to the network. Two of the young Californians who built and sold “blue boxes” to generate those tones were named Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs. A few years later they built a different box, in a garage, and called it Apple.

That lineage matters, because it tells you something the headlines never will: the exploratory, rule-bending, “I-wonder-what-happens-if” impulse that frightens people is the exact same impulse that built the industry those people now depend on. When 2600: The Hacker Quarterly took its name in 1984, it was naming itself after that whistle. The magazine still runs, and its founder — who writes under the pen name Emmanuel Goldstein, borrowed from the dissident in Orwell’s Nineteen Eighty-Four — has spent four decades insisting that curiosity is not a crime.

Part II — The Ethic

Somebody finally wrote it down

For twenty-five years the hacker ethic was unwritten, because inside the MIT labs it did not need defending. In 1984, the journalist Steven Levy wrote it down in Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution — still the single best book to hand a newcomer. Levy distilled the culture into a set of principles, including the “Hands-On Imperative” (you learn by taking things apart) and the one line that has caused more argument than any other in computing:

“All information should be free.”

The MIT hacker ethic, as documented in Steven Levy, Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution, 1984

You do not have to agree with that sentence — I don’t, not without a dozen caveats a defender learns the hard way — to understand what it was reaching for: a world where systems can be inspected, understood, and improved by the people who use them. That is an auditability argument, and it is the philosophical great-grandparent of everything from open source to responsible disclosure.

The document the whole culture memorized

Two years later, in January 1986, a nineteen-year-old who called himself The Mentor — the writer Loyd Blankenship — was arrested, sat down, and wrote a single page for the underground magazine Phrack. He titled it “The Conscience of a Hacker.” Everyone just calls it the Hacker Manifesto. If you spend any time in this community you will hear it quoted at cons, printed on shirts, and read aloud at memorials. It opens with the frustration of a smart kid treated as a criminal for being curious, and it ends like this:

“This is our world now… the world of the electron and the switch, the beauty of the baud.…

I am a hacker, and this is my manifesto. You may stop this individual, but you can’t stop us all… after all, we’re all alike.”

The Mentor (Loyd Blankenship), “The Conscience of a Hacker,” Phrack, Vol. 1, Issue 7, 8 January 1986

The manifesto’s most important sentence, for a newcomer, is the one about the writer’s actual crime: judging people by what they say and think, not by what they look like. That is the community at its best — a meritocracy of the curious, where a teenager and a Fortune 500 CISO argue as equals because the only currency that matters is whether your idea works.

What the mindset actually is

If you want the modern, precise version of what a hacker is — stripped of romance — the cryptographer Bruce Schneier has given it. A hacker, he writes, is someone who, when told a system works a certain way, immediately asks how it stops working if you do something else. A hack is any means of subverting a system’s rules in unintended ways while still operating inside the system.

“Security is a mindset. It’s a way of thinking about the world.”

Bruce Schneier, on the security mindset (see A Hacker’s Mind, 2023, and “What Is a Hacker?”, 2006)

Notice that nothing in that definition mentions computers, crime, or hoodies. The hacker mindset applies to tax codes, card games, and casino loyalty programs just as cleanly as to a login form. The mindset is morally neutral. What you do with it is the entire story — and it is the story the rest of this piece is about.

Part III — The Tribe

A newcomer’s map to where the community gathers

The culture is not online — not really. It is a travelling circus of conferences, local meetups, and all-night villages, and the fastest way to understand it is to go. Here is the map I give people, from the biggest tent to the smallest.

DEF CON — the capital city

DEF CON began in 1993 as a going-away party. An eighteen-year-old named Jeff Moss — handle The Dark Tangent — had planned a send-off for a friend who ran a hacker bulletin-board network, the friend had to leave town, and rather than cancel, Moss invited everyone he knew from every board he could reach. About a hundred people turned up at a Las Vegas hotel with no schedule and a tape recorder for a PA system. Today it fills multiple casinos and draws tens of thousands. Moss defines the word plainly:

Hacking is “curiosity plus skills.” It is a skill set — it’s neutral. And hackers are “civil society speaking truth to power.”

Jeff Moss (The Dark Tangent), founder of DEF CON and Black Hat, as reported from SecTor 2021

DEF CON is also where you learn the culture’s sense of humour and its complicated romance with the government. Moss invented “Spot the Fed,” a game where an attendee who thinks they’ve identified an undercover federal agent can call it out; if the crowd agrees, everyone goes home with a t-shirt. The feds, for their part, show up to recruit — because this is where the talent is. The social contract of the whole event is summarized in the phrase you will see on every sign: be excellent to each other. It is not a decoration. It is a code of conduct, and the goons (the red-shirted volunteer staff) will enforce it.

For your first con

Three rules that will save you. One: treat every network as hostile — DEF CON famously runs the “Wall of Sheep,” which publicly displays credentials sniffed off people dumb enough to log in over the open Wi-Fi. Two: ask before you photograph anyone; a lot of excellent people have very good reasons not to be on camera. Three: the hallway is the conference. The talks are recorded; the conversations are not. Go to the villages, pick up a lockpick, lose a game of Capture the Flag, and talk to people.

Security BSides — the B-side of the record

In 2009, Black Hat received more talk submissions than it had room for, and a group of strong speakers were turned away — not for quality, but for space. A conversation on Twitter turned into a plan, and Mike Dahn, Jack Daniel, and Chris Nickerson rented a house and ran the rejected talks themselves. They named it Security BSides, after the flip side of a vinyl single — the track that wasn’t the hit but was often better. It has since become a global, free-or-cheap, community-run network of events in cities all over the world. BSides is almost always the friendliest room a newcomer can walk into, and in most cities it is where you will give your own first talk.

ShmooCon — the one on the East Coast

ShmooCon ran from 2005 through its final event in 2025, organized by The Shmoo Group in Washington, DC. Founder Bruce Potter tells the origin story as a fist-bump: he and a co-founder sat through a Black Hat talk full of dubious claims, waited for someone in the audience to challenge the speaker, and when nobody did, decided to build a conference where someone would. ShmooCon drew a distinctly Beltway mix — researchers, academics, lawyers, and policy people — and its tickets sold out in seconds every year, a small masterpiece of community demand.

DerbyCon — the family

If you ask veterans which con they miss most, a startling number will say DerbyCon, which ran in Louisville, Kentucky from 2011 to 2019. It was co-founded by Dave Kennedy (handle ReL1K, author of the Social-Engineer Toolkit) along with Martin Bos, Adrian “Irongeek” Crenshaw, and others. When Kennedy announced the final year, he did not talk about talks or sponsors. He talked about what the conference had actually built:

DerbyCon was about “family, community and friendship” — and the family feel was always the most important part.

Dave Kennedy (ReL1K), co-founder, reflecting on DerbyCon’s close in 2019

That word — family — is the one you will hear most once you’re inside. These are people who met as teenagers on IRC, grew up, got married, hired each other, buried friends, and raised kids who now attend the same cons. It is a real community, with all the warmth and all the drama that implies.

SkyDogCon, and the long tail of regional cons

SkyDogCon, started in Nashville in 2011, bills itself as a hacker-and-maker conference — smaller, weirder, and more hands-on, with a beginner-friendly Capture the Flag that has introduced a lot of people to their first real challenge. It is one of dozens: nearly every region has its own now — CarolinaCon, CypherCon, Wild West Hackin’ Fest, CactusCon, and on and on. Find the one nearest you. The regional cons are where the community is most itself.

2600 meetings — the oldest ritual

You do not need a plane ticket to start. On the first Friday of every month, in cities all over the world, readers of 2600 gather in a public place — historically a food court or a lobby, somewhere anyone can walk up — for a 2600 meeting. They are free, open to anyone, and deliberately un-exclusive. The magazine also runs the biennial Hackers on Planet Earth (HOPE) conference in New York. If there is a 2600 meeting near you, that is the lowest-stakes door into this entire world.

OWASP — the professional on-ramp

If the cons are the culture, OWASP is the trade guild. The Open Worldwide Application Security Project (formerly Open Web Application Security Project) was started by Mark Curphey on 9 September 2001 and became a nonprofit foundation in 2004. Its Top Ten — a ranked list of the most critical web-application risks, first published in 2003 — is the single most-referenced document in application security, cited in contracts, compliance standards, and job descriptions worldwide. OWASP runs local chapters in over a hundred countries, and the meetings are free. For someone trying to turn the hacker mindset into a paycheck, your local OWASP chapter is the most useful room in town: it is full of the people who will eventually hire you, and the barrier to entry is showing up.

National Day of Civic Hacking — hacking for your neighbors

And then there is the kind of hacking that has nothing to do with breaking in. The National Day of Civic Hacking, launched in 2013 by Code for America with the U.S. Small Business Administration and SecondMuse, treats “hacking” in its oldest MIT sense: rapid, collaborative building for the public good. The first one was, at the time, the largest hackathon in history — more than 11,000 people across 83 cities, building tools to make government data usable, reduce littering, and make local law machine-readable. It is the clearest living proof that the word was never about crime. It was always about building.

Part IV — The Name

Why “hacker” raises eyebrows — and opens doors

Here is the practical question the title of this piece is really about. You can introduce yourself as a security consultant, an information security analyst, a penetration tester — all safe, all boring, all true. Or you can say the word hacker, and watch what happens to the room.

With a general-counsel type, a procurement officer, a nervous client: the word is a red flag. It triggers thirty years of movie posters and news chyrons. You will spend the next ten minutes explaining that you are not going to steal their identity. Say “consultant” to those people.

But with the right people — the engineer who actually runs the system, the researcher two desks over, the grizzled incident responder — the word is a password. It says you are one of them, that you think in exploits and edge cases, that you will tell them the uncomfortable truth their vendors won’t. I have been handed access, war stories, and unpublished findings inside of a minute because I used the honest word instead of the corporate one. The word gets you into rooms, and into confidences, that “consultant” never will.

“Consultant” is what you put on the invoice. “Hacker” is what gets you the information the invoice is for.

Learning which word to use in which room is a real skill, and it is worth developing. But understand what the word actually signals to the people who know it. It does not signal that you break the law. It signals the opposite of a specific thing — and that thing is the color of your hat.

This is the most important paragraph in the article, so read it twice. The skills are identical across all three hats. The white hat and the black hat run the same tools, read the same papers, attend the same talks. The only difference — the entire difference — is permission. The white hat has a signed contract and a scope. The black hat has neither, by choice. Everything else in your life as a hacker — whether you get paid or prosecuted, cleared or indicted, hired at forty or hiding at forty — follows from which side of that single line you decide to stand on.

Part V — The Better Angels

The kind-hearted hacker is not a myth. It is the majority.

The press covers the breaches, so you would be forgiven for thinking the community is mostly criminals. It is not. The overwhelming majority of the people you will meet are defenders, fixers, teachers, and volunteers, and the culture has institutionalized that generosity in ways most industries never bother to.

Start with the quiet act that defines the white hat: finding a stranger’s security bug and telling them, for free, so they can fix it before someone evil finds it. This is responsible disclosure, and an entire profession grew up around making it safe and rewarded. The pioneer is Katie Moussouris, who built Microsoft’s first bug-bounty program and then helped the U.S. Department of Defense launch “Hack the Pentagon” in 2016 — the first time the federal government formally invited hackers to break its systems and paid them for the holes they found. A generation of young researchers now makes an honest, excellent living this way, turning curiosity directly into a paycheck with no law broken.

Hackers for Charity

Then there is the hacker who gives the skill away entirely. In 2009, Johnny Long — already famous for writing the book on “Google hacking” (using ordinary search queries to find exposed systems) — moved his family to Jinja, Uganda, and founded Hackers for Charity. The idea is simple and beautiful: small charities in the developing world need technical help they cannot possibly afford, and hackers have exactly that skill sitting idle. HFC connects the two. Long built computer-training centers, networks, and a workspace, and funded it in part by donating the proceeds of his own Black Hat talks and books. His organization’s motto — “I Hack Charities” — is the whole philosophy in three words.

I Am The Cavalry

By 2013, as cars, pacemakers, and insulin pumps filled up with software, a group of researchers led by Josh Corman and Nick Percoco realized that nobody was coming to make these life-or-death systems safe — so they would have to. They founded I Am The Cavalry, a volunteer movement focused on the places where digital security meets human life: medical devices, cars, home systems, and public infrastructure. Their rallying line is the cleanest statement of the defender’s creed I know:

“The Cavalry isn’t coming. It falls to us.”

The founding ethos of I Am The Cavalry, born at DEF CON and BSides Las Vegas, 2013

And the lawyers who defend the curious

The community even defends itself in court. In 1990, after the U.S. Secret Service raided a small game publisher, Steve Jackson Games, during a hacking dragnet — seizing computers and an unpublished book — John Perry Barlow, Mitch Kapor, and John Gilmore founded the Electronic Frontier Foundation to make sure the law treated hackers as citizens with rights rather than suspects by default. The EFF has been the community’s legal conscience ever since, and the Steve Jackson Games case established that your email doesn’t lose its protection just because a federal agent finds hacking scary.

Security reading

The “better angels” are not a PR exercise. They are the economic and moral core of the field. Every bug bounty, every free OWASP chapter meeting, every volunteer teaching a kid to pick a lock at a village table is the community doing, at scale, the thing the original MIT definition described: understanding a system deeply enough to make it better. When you hear someone say hackers are dangerous, the honest answer is that they are the people paid to be dangerous to your attackers.

Part VI — The Underbelly

Now the part nobody likes to say out loud

I would be lying to you, and you’d catch me, if I pretended the dark side was small or far away. It is neither. There is a thriving criminal economy wearing the same word we do, and some of the people in it are very good at what they do. A newcomer needs to see it clearly — both to resist it and to recognize it.

Start with the uncomfortable truth about money. Crime pays better, for a while. Industry research on cybercriminal economics has put the top operators’ earnings in the millions a year — CEO money — and even mid-tier players well into six figures, often two to three times what the equivalent legitimate role pays. Ransomware crews run like startups, with developers, support desks, and affiliate programs. The black hats know this, and some of them sneer at the white hats for it: for taking the smaller paycheck, for “asking permission,” for playing a game they consider rigged and naive. You will meet that contempt. Do not mistake it for wisdom. It is the reasoning of people who have not yet been caught.

The sneer, answered

The black-hat economics are real, and they are also a trap with a time limit. The income is untaxed, unbankable, and unspendable without laundering; the wallets are traceable; the crew contains at least one person who will become an informant the moment a prosecutor offers them a deal; and the clock never stops. The sneer at white hats — “you work for scraps” — leaves out the part where the white hat is still working, and free, at fifty. A smaller number that arrives every two weeks for forty years beats a bigger number that ends in a courtroom.

The blight that is not hacking at all

And then there is the worst of it — the part that has nothing to do with curiosity, skill, or any version of the word we would claim. A small population of genuine predators uses the same networks, the same anonymity tools, and the same forums to produce and trade child sexual abuse material. In my judgment these are the worst criminals alive, and I want to be unambiguous about where the community stands: they are not hackers. They are not “grey.” They are not edgy. They are a blight on a vibrant culture, and the culture despises them.

This is not a soft consensus. Legitimate forums, conferences, and platforms ban this content on sight and report it to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children and law enforcement; con codes of conduct exist in part to keep predators out of community spaces; and some of the most dedicated people I have ever met work the defensive side of this problem — building detection, assisting investigators, and burning out quietly so that children they will never meet are a little safer. If you ever find yourself in a room where this is tolerated, you are not in the hacker community. You are in a criminal space that stole our vocabulary. Leave, and report it.

The people who share that filth did not join our community. They are hiding inside it. Hand them to the police and never once call them by our name.

Part VII — The Ledger

Two careers, built from the identical skill

Let me show you what the choice in Part IV actually looks like, using two real people who started with the same abilities and ended in very different places.

Kevin Mitnick was the most wanted hacker in America in the 1990s — a social-engineering savant who talked his way into systems that firewalls could not keep him out of. He was caught, he went to prison, and then he did the thing that defines the honorable path: he came out and went legit. He built a successful security-consulting firm, wrote the field’s best books on social engineering, and sold out keynote stages as a defender until his death in 2023. His most-quoted line is the whole of security in nine words:

“Social engineering bypasses all technologies, including firewalls.”

“Companies spend millions of dollars on firewalls… and it’s money wasted, because none of these measures address the weakest link in the security chain: the people.”

Kevin Mitnick (1963–2023), reformed hacker and author of The Art of Deception

Now the counter-example. Albert Gonzalez had the same gift and made the opposite choice. Using SQL injection and packet-sniffing, he and his crew stole more than 170 million payment-card numbers from TJX, Heartland, and others — at the time the largest such theft in history. The detail you must not miss: after an early arrest, Gonzalez became a Secret Service informant — and secretly kept running his hacks the whole time. In 2010 he was sentenced to 20 years in federal prison. Same skills as Mitnick. Different hat. Two decades gone.

The arrest that should haunt every talented kid

If Gonzalez feels like a cartoon villain, here is the one that should actually frighten you, because he was almost you. Marcus Hutchins — handle MalwareTech — is the researcher who, in May 2017, found and triggered the “kill switch” that stopped the WannaCry ransomware worm as it tore through hospitals worldwide. Overnight he was a global hero. Three months later, as he tried to fly home from DEF CON, the FBI arrested him at the Las Vegas airport — for malware he had written as a teenager, years before. He pleaded guilty, and in 2019 a judge, weighing the good against the old wrong, sentenced him to time served. He got lucky. The lesson is not that it works out. The lesson is that the thing you build at seventeen can be waiting for you at the airport at twenty-three, on your way home from the best week of your year.

The do’s

  1. Get permission in writing, every single time. Scope, dates, systems, and a signature. The permission is the difference between a pentest and a felony. No exceptions, not for a “quick look,” not for a friend.
  2. Build a lab you own. Break your own machines, your own VMs, deliberately vulnerable targets (OWASP’s WebGoat, Hack The Box, TryHackMe, the SkyDogCon CTF). Unlimited learning, zero legal risk.
  3. Learn responsible disclosure and use it. Found a stranger’s bug? Report it through their program or the EFF’s guidance. Collect the bounty. That is the white-hat paycheck, and it is real.
  4. Show up in person. A local 2600 meeting, an OWASP chapter, a BSides. The community hires from the hallway, not the résumé pile.
  5. Protect the anonymity of others. Ask before you photograph. Don’t out people. “Be excellent to each other” is the actual rule.
  6. Aim at the clearance. A clean record plus real skill is a scarce, valuable, government-and-defense-grade combination. Guard your record like the asset it is.

The don’ts

  • Don’t touch a system you weren’t invited to touch. Not to “just look,” not to prove a point. In the United States the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act does not care about your good intentions.
  • Don’t let anyone talk you into “grey.” Unsolicited access is a crime even when you mean to help. Good intentions have sent plenty of people to court.
  • Don’t chase the black-hat money. It is bigger, briefly, and then it is an indictment with your handle on it. The wallet is traceable and the crew will flip.
  • Don’t keep what you built when you were reckless. Old code, old handles, old boasts — Hutchins learned at an airport that the past does not expire on its own.
  • Don’t ever tolerate predators in a community space. CSAM, stalking, doxxing to cause harm — leave, and report it. That is not our culture and never was.
Take the smaller cheque. Stay out of jail. Keep the clearance. Still be hireable at forty. That is the entire game.

The Film You Should Watch Tonight

Watch — free, and officially released

DEF CON: The Documentary (2013)

If you read only one thing and watch only one thing before your first con, make it this. In 2012 the archivist and filmmaker Jason Scott — of the Internet Archive and textfiles.com — was given extraordinary access to shoot DEF CON’s 20th anniversary. The result premiered on the opening day of DEF CON 21 in August 2013, and — in a move that is itself pure hacker ethic — DEF CON released it for free, as a legal torrent, on Vimeo and YouTube, and on the Internet Archive with subtitles.

It is the best ninety minutes you can spend to understand the feel of this world: the goons, the villages, the badge hacking, the all-nighters, and above all the people — nervous first-timers and twenty-year veterans, filmed with real affection by someone who is unmistakably one of them. It captures the thing this whole article is trying to describe and can’t quite: that for all the talk of exploits and economics, the hacker community is, at bottom, a bunch of curious people who finally found each other.

Where to watch: search “DEF CON The Documentary” on YouTube (official DEFCONConference channel), or stream it with subtitles on the Internet Archive at archive.org/details/2013_DEFCON_Documentary.

The through-line

Almost thirty years in, here is what I actually believe. The word hacker was ours first — coined as a compliment in an MIT clubroom, carried by phone phreaks and free-software pioneers and the volunteers who hack for charities and cities, defended in the Senate and in court, and celebrated every summer in the Nevada desert by a community that calls itself a family and means it. A small number of criminals borrowed the word to make themselves sound interesting, and the press let them keep it. We don’t have to.

You have the mindset or you wouldn’t have read this far. The only decision left is the hat. Choose the one that lets you keep doing this at fifty, in daylight, with your real name on the badge. Then come find us. We saved you a seat.

We were never the villains of the story. We were the ones who stayed up all night to read it.

On quotations. Where a line appears inside quotation marks, it is a direct quote drawn from the cited source or a verified public record of it; where a view is attributed without quotation marks (for example, several of the founders’ positions on community), it is a faithful paraphrase of their documented public statements, not a verbatim transcript. I have erred toward paraphrase whenever I could not confirm exact wording, because a culture that cites its sources should not invent its own quotations.

On the artwork, and why there are no photographs of people. The cover and all three diagrams are original vector work by Savvy Security LLC. There are deliberately no photographs of the individuals named here. This blog’s standing practice is to use portraits only from Creative Commons sources with the photographer and licence verified and credited — and in the environment where this piece was produced, that verification was not possible. Rather than publish images with attribution I could not confirm (a real legal and ethical risk on a commercial site), I chose original art and let the subjects speak in their own words instead. Properly licensed, correctly credited portraits can be added later; borrowed ones should not.
