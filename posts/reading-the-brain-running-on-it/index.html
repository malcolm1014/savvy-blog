Reading the Brain, and Running On It

A century of electroencephalography, the AI inflection point, and the arrival of data centers made of human neurons.

By Malcolm R. Smith — Savvy Security LLC

Two research programs that spent decades in separate buildings have arrived, within the same eighteen months, at commercial deployment. The first reads electrical activity off the human scalp and, using large self-supervised models, converts it into text, states, and inferences at accuracies that were considered out of reach in 2020. The second grows human neurons from donated stem cells, wires them to electrode arrays, and sells the result as computing infrastructure — in August 2026, a twenty-unit rack of living neural tissue was formally commissioned as a working data center prototype in Singapore.

This article treats them as a single subject, because they share a substrate, a supply chain, an economic logic, and — critically — a regulatory vacuum. It covers the biophysics of the EEG signal and its hard limits; what a century of electrophysiology has established that scalp electrodes can and cannot detect; the deep-learning inflection that changed the second half of that answer; the migration of EEG out of the clinic into earbuds and headphones; the emerging patchwork of neural-data law; and the state of wetware computing, including an honest accounting of its energy claims. It closes on the ethical symmetry the two fields create: an industry built on neural data taken from living people, and an industry built on neural tissue grown from their cells.

I. Introduction: The Same Voltage, Twice

The electrical potential that an EEG electrode measures and the electrical potential that a biological computer manipulates are the same physical quantity, produced by the same cellular mechanism. In one case, the neurons are inside a person's skull and the measurement is an act of observation. In the other, the neurons are in a temperature-controlled microfluidic chamber and the measurement is an act of computation.

This is not a rhetorical parallel. It is an engineering fact with consequences. The multi-electrode arrays used in organoid computing are direct descendants of the electrophysiology rigs that characterized cortical rhythms; the amplifiers, the sampling regimes, and much of the signal-processing literature are shared. When the field says "wetware," it is describing the same instrument pointed at tissue that was never in a head.

That shared lineage is why the two developments belong in one analysis. The consumer-neurotechnology industry is producing continuous streams of neural data from millions of people, largely outside medical regulation. The biocomputing industry is producing computational substrate from donated human cells, largely outside any regulation at all. Both are scaling. Neither has settled governance. And the questions they raise — what a neural signal reveals, what a neural tissue is owed — turn out to be the same question approached from opposite ends.

II. The Physics of a Scalp Voltage

Berger's problem

Hans Berger recorded the first human electroencephalogram in 1924 and published in 1929, and the delay was not modesty. Berger did not believe his own data for five years. He was looking, initially, for a physical basis for telepathy — a fact the field has largely elected to forget — and what he found instead was the alpha rhythm: a roughly 8–12 Hz oscillation that appeared when his subjects closed their eyes and vanished when they opened them. His contemporaries did not credit the work until Edgar Adrian, a Nobel laureate, replicated it in 1934.

Berger's caution was appropriate. The signal he had found is extraordinarily indirect, and understanding exactly how indirect is the necessary precondition for evaluating every claim made about EEG in the century since.

What actually generates the signal

Scalp EEG does not measure neurons firing. This is the single most persistent misconception in popular coverage of the technology, and correcting it removes most of the magic.

Action potentials — the discrete spikes that constitute a neuron's output — are too brief and too spatially incoherent to sum into anything detectable at the scalp. What EEG measures instead is postsynaptic potentials from pyramidal neurons in the cerebral cortex: not individual neuron firings, but the summed electrical activity of millions of neurons firing in synchrony.

The geometry is what makes it possible at all. Cortical pyramidal cells are arranged in parallel sheets with their apical dendrites oriented perpendicular to the cortical surface. Because those apical dendrites run parallel to one another, the excitatory postsynaptic potentials in them sum across space and time and can be approximated as a current dipole — positive ions entering the dendrite create an extracellular sink at one end and a corresponding source at the other. Millions of these microscopic dipoles, aligned and synchronized, produce a macroscopic field.

These neurons are oriented normal to the cortex and are commonly activated synchronously by groups of tens of thousands of neurons. The minimum electrically active patch of cortex that registers reliably at the scalp is on the order of a square centimeter — while roughly 100 electrodes suffice to represent the scalp potential field, the number of possible source positions within the head is around 10,000.

Two consequences follow immediately, and everything else about EEG is downstream of them.

Consequence one: the signal is a synchrony detector, not a content reader. A thought that does not recruit a large, spatially aligned, temporally synchronized population of cortical pyramidal cells produces no scalp signature at all. EEG is systematically blind to computation that is distributed, asynchronous, deep, or geometrically unfavorable — which is to say, most of it.

Consequence two: the inverse problem has no unique solution. Given a set of scalp voltages, there is no mathematically determined answer to the question of what generated them. Since the solution to an inverse problem is not unique, solving it requires beginning with a set of constraining assumptions about how the signal is generated — typically that sources lie on the cortical surface and are perpendicular to it. Any inverse solution depends critically on the chosen constraints: one or two isolated sources, distributed sources confined to cortex, or spatial and temporal smoothness criteria. Different reasonable assumptions produce different reasonable answers. Source localization is inference, not measurement.

Volume conduction and the skull problem

Between the cortical dipole and the electrode sit the cerebrospinal fluid, the dura, the skull, and the scalp. The signal must pass through cerebrospinal fluid, skull bone, and scalp tissue before reaching the electrode, which weakens and blurs it through volume conduction; each layer smears and distorts the signal, making it difficult to pinpoint the source. Skull conductivity is roughly two orders of magnitude lower than that of the tissues on either side of it, which makes the skull a spatial low-pass filter. It is the fundamental reason EEG's spatial resolution is what it is, and no amount of additional electrodes fully defeats it.

The trade is well understood: EEG has far superior temporal resolution, capturing changes in milliseconds rather than seconds. Millisecond timing, centimeter-scale localization. Every application of the technology is an attempt to extract value from that specific bargain.

The canonical decomposition

Clinical and research EEG conventionally partitions the signal into frequency bands — delta (roughly 0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8), alpha (8–13), beta (13–30), gamma (above 30) — with the 10-20 electrode placement system, standardized by Herbert Jasper in 1958, providing anatomically referenced positions (Fp1, Cz, O2, TP9, and so on) that make recordings comparable across labs and decades.

It is worth naming that this decomposition is a convention rather than a discovery. Band boundaries are historical artifacts, band power is not a mechanism, and a substantial contemporary literature argues that much of what gets analyzed as oscillatory band power is in fact the aperiodic 1/f component of the spectrum shifting — a confound that has quietly contaminated an unknown fraction of the applied EEG literature, including work that consumer devices now cite as validation.

III. What a Century of Electrophysiology Established

Event-related potentials

Averaging the EEG across many repetitions of a stimulus cancels uncorrelated background activity and reveals time-locked components. This technique — the event-related potential — is the workhorse of cognitive electrophysiology and the basis of nearly every practical brain-computer interface.

The reliable components are few and well characterized. The P300 is a positive deflection roughly 300 ms after a rare or salient stimulus, and it is robust enough that it underpins the P300 speller, the most-replicated BCI in existence. The N400 is a negative deflection around 400 ms that scales with semantic incongruity — the electrophysiological signature of a word that does not fit its context. The mismatch negativity indexes automatic detection of deviance in a stimulus stream and is measurable in unconscious patients, which makes it clinically valuable in disorders of consciousness.

Note what these have in common: each is an evoked, averaged response to a stimulus whose timing the experimenter controls. Remove the experimental control and most of the reliability goes with it.

The readiness potential, and the argument it started

In 1965, Kornhuber and Deecke identified a slow negative shift in the EEG preceding voluntary movement. The readiness potential is a buildup of neural activity in motor areas beginning approximately 500 ms before voluntary movement.

In 1983, Libet had participants perform a voluntary finger movement whenever they felt the urge while watching a clock dial to report the moment they first felt the intention. The readiness potential appeared about 550 ms before movement; participants reported conscious intention only about 200 ms before. The apparent implication — that unconscious brain activity initiates action before the person knows they have decided — became one of the most cited results in cognitive science and the empirical centerpiece of a generation of arguments against free will.

It is also, at minimum, seriously contested, and this matters enormously for how one should read contemporary claims about "predicting intent."

A major reinterpretation emerged in 2012 when Aaron Schurger proposed an accumulator model: the readiness potential may not signify an intention to act but rather the stochastic buildup of neural activity, with movement triggered when that activity randomly crosses a threshold — reframing the RP as a byproduct of neural noise rather than an intentional signal. The stochastic decision model, inspired by leaky accumulator models of perceptual decision making, suggests the RP originates from an accumulation of ongoing stochastic fluctuations, and it has the attractive property of removing the counterintuitive delay: it implies the decision to act occurs late, when the accumulated signal crosses threshold, much closer to the subjective time of decision.

The debate has not resolved. A 2025 study using a probe-based paradigm found that pre-probe RP buildups were not related to reported awareness of motor preparation, with computational modeling suggesting the best explanation is metacognitive access to stochastic accumulation. And critically for anyone imagining pre-emptive intervention: Schultze-Kraft and colleagues showed in 2016 that subjects could veto actions if a stop cue appeared early enough — brain activity may prepare for movement, but conscious intervention can still occur at a late stage.

The honest summary is this: there is a measurable neural antecedent to spontaneous movement, its interpretation is genuinely unsettled after sixty years, and the popular framing — the machine knows before you do — rests on the weaker of two competing readings of a contested effect. This should be the default posture toward any commercial claim of intent prediction.

State decoding: where EEG genuinely delivers

Against the difficulty of decoding content, EEG is very good at decoding state. It is considered the gold standard for sleep staging and epilepsy diagnosis. Drowsiness, vigilance decrement, anesthetic depth, cognitive workload, and seizure activity all produce large, distributed, synchronous changes — precisely the class of phenomenon the physics favors.

This asymmetry is the most useful single heuristic for evaluating neurotechnology claims. EEG reads states well and contents poorly, because states are synchronous and contents are not.

IV. The AI Inflection

From handcrafted features to foundation models

For most of its history, EEG analysis meant expert-designed features: band powers, common spatial patterns, Riemannian covariance methods. Early EEG decoding studies predominantly employed traditional machine learning methods that depend on handcrafted features requiring substantial prior knowledge and generalizing weakly.

Deep learning changed the pipeline, and then changed the paradigm. Convolutional architectures gave way to hybrids: the EEG Conformer integrates convolution and transformer layers into a unified architecture that extracts local information while capturing global structure in EEG signals. Then came pretraining at scale. BENDR used contrastive self-supervised learning to learn generic EEG representations from massive unlabeled data, addressing the chronic problem of limited labeled EEG, and a rapid succession of foundation models followed — LaBraM, EEGPT, CBraMod, Neuro-GPT, EEGMamba, LUNA — culminating in models pretrained across enormous heterogeneous corpora, including REVE, a foundation model designed to adapt to any recording setup, pretrained on 25,000 subjects.

The rationale is explicit in the literature. Neural signals remain difficult to decode due to high dimensionality, nonstationarity, and substantial interindividual variability, and traditional models show limited generalizability; foundation models pretrained on diverse data have emerged as a promising paradigm, with EEG serving as the most practical platform for their development due to large open datasets, standardized protocols, and broad clinical applicability.

Two things are worth flagging. First, evaluation in this subfield is not yet mature: a critical review notes cases where models showed generalizability to unseen subjects and electrode locations, but the test data came from the same distribution as the training data. Second, a 2026 spectral audit framework found that deep models on EEG and ECG frequently rely on the aperiodic component of the signal rather than the oscillatory features their authors assume — meaning strong benchmark performance does not establish that a model has learned what its designers believe it has learned.

The Brain2Qwerty results, read carefully

The most consequential recent demonstration in non-invasive decoding is Meta's Brain2Qwerty, and it deserves precision because it is routinely misreported.

In the original study, 35 participants typed briefly memorized sentences while recorded with EEG or MEG; the three-stage model combined a convolutional module over 500 ms windows, a sentence-level transformer, and a pretrained language model to correct the transformer's outputs. Published in Nature Neuroscience in 2026, the headline figures were: with magnetoencephalography, a character error rate of 29% on average, substantially outperforming electroencephalography at 65%; for the best participants, 18%.

A second generation followed. Brain2Qwerty v2, trained on 22,000 sentences from nine subjects each recorded for ten hours, achieved an average word error rate of 39%, with the best participant's sentences decoded with one word error or less half the time. Trained on 10× more data per participant, v2 reaches up to 78% word accuracy for the best participant.

Four caveats govern the interpretation, and Meta states most of them itself.

  1. The good numbers are MEG, not EEG. The EEG condition performed dramatically worse — 65% CER versus 29%. Magnetoencephalography is not blocked by the skull the way volume-conducted current is, and that difference is physical, not algorithmic.
  2. MEG is a room, not a wearable. The device is a large scanner, a setup inaccessible to most patients, though optically pumped magnetometers may eventually change this.
  3. A language model is doing substantial work. Decoding accuracy is partly a function of how predictable the typed content is; this is a feature for assistive communication and a confound for any claim about reading unpredictable thought.
  4. The task is overt typing. Participants moved their fingers. Much of the decodable signal is motor.

That last point connects to the most important negative result in the field. A 2025 doctoral thesis comparing modalities found that silent reading of words could be decoded from EEG, MEG, and OPMs at 30–40% accuracy across five words, driven by early visual processing — but inner speech decoding was mostly at chance across multiple approaches and both modalities. Extensive effort to decode two types of inner speech never substantially exceeded chance levels.

Non-invasive brain-to-text works when there is a motor or sensory event to anchor it. Decoding unspoken inner language remains, as of this writing, unachieved.

The genuine cause for attention is the scaling behavior. Results follow a scaling law: more training data yields better decoders without — at least so far — a detectable performance plateau. Decoding accuracy improves log-linearly with data volume, suggesting the gap with intracranial approaches could be partially bridged through data scaling. Log-linear improvement means each increment costs exponentially more data — but it also means the ceiling has not been found, and the field's constraint has shifted from algorithms to recording hours.

V. Out of the Clinic

The gel-and-cap era is ending. Wearable EEG is migrating from bulky gel-based clinical systems to consumer-ready, dry-electrode, and ear-centered form factors supporting continuous real-world monitoring, driven by advances in biosensor hardware, wireless transmission, and on-device signal processing.

The engineering problem in dry electrodes is contact impedance without conductive gel. The central innovation is electrode geometry — comb, spring-loaded, or foam-tipped designs that penetrate hair and maintain scalp contact — with signal quality comparisons against clinical gold-standard systems consistently showing high cross-correlation for resting-state and evoked-response tasks.

The more consequential development is the ear. Ear-EEG seeks to minimize device visibility and maximize long-term wearability, with electrodes embedded in custom earpieces or behind-the-ear mounts, exploiting the ear canal's proximity to temporal cortex sources. The form factor matters more than the signal quality: an earbud is socially invisible, worn for hours, and already normalized.

By CES 2026 this had become a product category. Neurable announced a headset with gaming brand HyperX intended to help players track focus via real-time brain activity, with its over-ear EEG headphones available for preorder; Naox brought in-ear EEG to consumer earbuds; and Elemind offered a sleep headband. NextSense closed a $16M Series A and released Smartbuds in early 2026 — true wireless earbuds using six EEG sensors, delivering soft pink noise in response to real-time brainwave data. IDUN Technologies pairs patented dry electrode technology with Analog Devices' biopotential front end in a brain-sensing earbud.

The clinical logic behind the sleep applications is sound. Most consumer sleep tracking measures indirectly, using peripheral signals such as movement, heart rate, and SpO2 — but sleep is fundamentally a brain state, and EEG measures REM and NREM directly, capturing signatures peripheral sensors cannot detect. The stated next step is closed-loop intervention, where EEG earbuds measure sleep through the night and deliver adaptive audio in real time to improve sleep metrics.

That phrase — closed-loop — is the hinge on which the entire ethical discussion turns, and it is worth stating plainly: the same hardware that records can stimulate, and the industry's own roadmap is to close the loop.

Market forecasts should be treated as the marketing artifacts they largely are, but the direction is consistent: the wearable EEG device market, valued at $685.4 million in 2026, is projected to reach roughly $2.09 billion by 2035 at a 13.2% CAGR, with headbands and ear-EEG the fastest-expanding tiers. Regulatory positioning is deliberate — the FDA classifies wearable EEG under varying tiers depending on diagnostic claims, creating a bifurcated pathway between wellness peripherals and regulated clinical tools. Most consumer devices are engineered, in their marketing language, to stay on the wellness side of that line.

VI. The Governance Gap

The audit

The empirical baseline for this discussion is a 2024 survey by the Neurorights Foundation. It found that the privacy practices of 29 out of 30 consumer neurotechnology companies appeared to allow access to consumers' neural data with no meaningful limitations. 96.7% of companies reserved the right to transfer brain data to third parties, with most policies vague on sale or brokerage, and inconsistent deletion or access rights.

In the United States, until Congress acts, neural data is regulated federally only by Section 5 of the FTC Act, which prohibits unfair or deceptive practices but does not require companies to post privacy policies or grant consumers rights to access, correct, or delete.

The state patchwork

Colorado moved first in 2024, folding neural data into "biological data" as a sensitive category. California followed: SB 1223 defined neural data as sensitive personal information under the CCPA, passing the Senate 38-0 and the Assembly 76-0, signed September 28, 2024, effective January 1, 2025. Montana amended its Genetic Information Privacy Act to include "neurotechnology data," passing 99-0 and 49-1 and taking effect October 1, 2025. Connecticut added a definition of neural data from central nervous system activity effective 2026.

The wave is continuing. A March 2026 analysis identified active neural data bills in Virginia, Alabama, California (a second measure targeting workplace surveillance), New York, Illinois, and Vermont, each taking a distinct regulatory approach.

The near-unanimous vote margins are the tell. Neural privacy is, so far, one of the few technology-policy questions in America without a partisan valence.

The international layer

On November 12, 2025, UNESCO's General Conference adopted its Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology — the first attempt at a global framework for the ethical development and use of neurotechnology, setting out a rights-based framework across the entire product life cycle. It is not legally binding, but its provisions are addressed to member states, research organizations, and private companies.

Its substantive positions are notably firm. It recognizes neural data as uniquely sensitive and calls for strict safeguards against misuse; it holds that individuals have the right to choose whether to use or be subject to neurotechnology at any time, with consent freely given and informed. It identifies particular risk for children and young people, whose brains are still developing, advising against non-therapeutic use; and it warns against workplace use to monitor productivity or build employee data profiles, insisting on explicit consent and full transparency. UNESCO's framing of the current landscape is blunt — it describes the sector outside medicine as largely unregulated, with many consumers using the technology without knowing it, via common devices such as connected headbands or headphones.

Elsewhere: Chile amended its constitution in 2021 to protect neurorights, the first nation to do so, and its Supreme Court has since applied those provisions in at least one case involving a consumer neurotechnology company. Brazil's state of Rio Grande do Sul followed in December 2023; Mexico introduced a 92-article General Law on Neurorights and Neurotechnologies in July 2024. In Europe, the AI Act partially covers neurotechnology by prohibiting AI systems using subliminal techniques to distort behavior, with guidelines explicitly referencing brain-computer interfaces as a vector — though the Act lacks neuro-specific risk classifications.

The workplace precedent

The workplace case is not hypothetical, and it is instructive precisely because the technology involved was weak.

Reporting beginning in 2018 documented Chinese firms deploying EEG sensors in workers' headgear. Employees in factories, in the military, and on trains were given hats and helmets equipped to monitor their brain waves; sensors were wirelessly embedded in the brims of electric company workers' helmets and train conductors' hats, monitoring for spikes and dips associated with panic, fatigue, and sadness, with some companies adding sensors to VR headsets to monitor trainees. One vendor claimed detection of fatigue and attention loss at better than 90% accuracy, with alarms to wake dozing conductors.

MIT Technology Review's assessment at the time remains the correct one. Over-the-skin brain scanning through EEG is still very limited in what it can detect, and the relationship between those signals and human emotion is not clear; a claimed $315 million profit boost from such monitoring is incredibly doubtful.

The lesson is not that the technology worked. It is that deployment does not wait for validity. A system that cannot reliably detect rage can still be used to justify assigning, disciplining, or dismissing a worker, and the asymmetry that a Beijing Normal University professor identified at the time is the durable one: the employer has a strong incentive to use the technology for profit, and employees are usually in too weak a position to refuse.

The inference problem

Existing law is mostly written about neural data. The harder object is the inference. A raw voltage trace is nearly meaningless; a model's probabilistic claim that a person is depressed, disengaged, deceptive, or drifting is highly consequential and may not be neural data under any of these statutes. As the Stanford Law School analysis frames it, the technology generates novel forms of data: neural signals and the probabilistic inferences derived from them, raising the foundational question of how law should conceptualize and regulate information that reveals, or purports to reveal, the contents of the human mind.

That phrase — or purports to reveal — is the most important four words in the current legal literature. An inference does not have to be accurate to be harmful. It has to be acted upon.

VII. The Other Direction: Computing With Neurons

From Pong to product

In 2022, Cortical Labs demonstrated DishBrain: a self-adapting computer 'brain' made by placing 800,000 human and mouse neurons on a chip and training the network to play Pong. The demonstration mattered less for the game than for the principle — that a cultured network could be placed in a closed sensorimotor loop and adapt.

The theoretical program was named the following year at Johns Hopkins. Thomas Hartung's team outlined "organoid intelligence" in Frontiers in Science, arguing that computing and AI are "reaching a ceiling" and that biocomputing represents an effort to compact computational power and increase its efficiency past current limits. The accompanying Baltimore Declaration called on the scientific community to explore human brain organoid cultures for new forms of biological computing while proactively addressing ethical concerns — including possible organoid consciousness and the rights of cell donors.

The scale involved is worth internalizing. Brain organoids under 500 μm in diameter comprising fewer than 100,000 cells are roughly one three-millionth the size of a human brain, and are typically generated from induced pluripotent stem cells, usually derived from skin samples — less ethically fraught than embryonic stem cells.

The two commercial platforms

FinalSpark (Switzerland, founded 2014) operates the Neuroplatform, an online platform providing remote access to 16 human brain organoids, which the company describes as the first delivering access to biological neurons in vitro. The architecture is straightforward electrophysiology: four multi-electrode arrays, each holding four organoids interfaced by eight electrodes used for both stimulation and recording, with data moving through an Intan RHS 32 controller at 30 kHz sampling and 16-bit resolution. Access was offered to institutions at $500 per user per month. Training uses chemical reward: dopamine is encapsulated in a molecular cage and released by specific light frequencies at precisely the intended moment. The operational record is substantial — over three years the Neuroplatform was used with more than 1,000 brain organoids, collecting more than 18 terabytes of data, with organoid lifetimes exceeding 100 days supported by a fully automated microfluidic medium-exchange system.

Cortical Labs (Australia, founded 2020) launched the CL1 at Mobile World Congress on March 2, 2025 — a self-contained biological computer integrating lab-grown human neurons on an electrode array within a housing that includes pumps, gas mixing, temperature control, and filtration to keep neurons alive for up to six months, priced at approximately US$35,000, drawing 850 to 1,000 watts, and requiring no external computer. Neuron counts reported for the unit range from 200,000 lab-grown brain cells wired to silicon circuits to 800,000 neurons on a silicon chip processing information through sub-millisecond feedback loops depending on configuration and source. The company markets it as an ethically superior alternative to animal testing that delivers more relevant human data.

Biological data centers

In March 2026 this moved from laboratory to infrastructure. Cortical Labs unveiled its first biological data center in Melbourne and announced a second in Singapore with partner DayOne Data Centers — facilities housing CL1 units powered by human brain cells rather than racks of conventional processors. The Melbourne facility is planned for 120 CL1 units; DayOne plans as many as 1,000 units in Singapore. DayOne provides capital and strategic input, with an initial validation phase of 20 units at the National University of Singapore's Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine.

That prototype went live in August 2026. On 6 August 2026, NUS Medicine, DayOne and Cortical Labs showcased the Biological Data Centre prototype at the NUS Life Sciences Institute to more than 80 guests from the private sector, academia, and the digital infrastructure sector. Cells are cultured under the supervision of Professor Rickie Patani, Professor of Neuroscience at NUS Medicine and Director of the Neurobiology Programme at the NUS Life Sciences Institute. The installation comprises twenty CL1 units, each holding at least 200,000 lab-grown neurons on an electrode-fitted silicon chip — roughly 4 million neurons, and the first server rack anywhere to use actual brain cells as its primary computing substrate rather than silicon designed to imitate them.

The institutional significance is greater than the technical. DayOne is a commercial data center operator with 2.1 gigawatts of confirmed bookings and a planned dual listing on Nasdaq and the Singapore Exchange, which closed a $4.5 billion round in June 2026 at a $20 billion valuation. A capitalized infrastructure operator embedding a wetware system into its development roadmap alongside conventional hyperscale facilities represents a different category of commitment than a university announcement — and notably, the prototype is not positioned as a replacement for GPU clusters.

The energy claim, audited

The entire commercial case rests on power. It requires scrutiny.

The framing figure is real and uncontroversial: a human brain runs about 20 watts. FinalSpark's co-founder extrapolates from it that bioprocessors could be up to a million times more energy-efficient than traditional silicon chips, and the company notes that training a model like GPT-3 required roughly 10 GWh, about 6,000 times the annual energy use of an average European citizen.

Now the accounting problem. Cortical Labs' comparison is that CL1 biological computers run on just 30W against silicon GPUs requiring up to 600W. But the CL1 as a shipping product uses 850 to 1,000 watts — because keeping neurons alive requires pumps, gas mixing, thermal regulation at body temperature, and filtration. The 30W figure describes the computational element; the ~900W figure describes the machine you plug in.

This is not necessarily dishonest — silicon comparisons routinely quote chip TDP rather than facility power including cooling — but it means the million-fold efficiency claim is a statement about neurons, not about biological computers as currently built. The life-support overhead is the dominant term today, and any credible efficiency argument depends on amortizing that overhead across vastly more neurons per unit.

The hard limits

The scaling barriers are physical, and the researchers involved are candid about them.

Vascularization is the wall. "Organoids do not have blood vessels — this is the biggest ongoing challenge," says Professor Simon Schultz of Imperial College London. Whereas blood vessels in human brains circulate nutrients continuously, lab-grown neurons lack vascular systems and there is no feasible way to create them; organoids are avascular and rely on passive diffusion, which imposes a hard ceiling on organoid diameter and therefore on complexity.

Maturity and architecture fall far short. Brain organoids still differ significantly from real brains in cell types and gene expression, regionalization and neural circuitry, developmental stage, and maturity — differences that affect the accuracy of their learning and memory processes.

There is no Moore's Law here. Unlike silicon, which benefits from exponential transistor density growth, wetware lacks predictable scaling pathways, with evaluations of organoid platforms indicating persistent reliance on hybrid architectures, and efficiency falters in high-throughput or precision-oriented workloads, where biological noise from stochastic firing and metabolic variability degrades reproducibility.

Lifespan is measured in months. Neurons in a CL1 are designed to remain alive up to six months. A data center whose compute substrate dies twice a year is a genuinely novel operations problem.

And the field itself is measured about the endpoint. Johns Hopkins scientists maintain that wetware "should complement, not replace, silicon AI" — a sentiment echoed across most of the research community. At present, wetware systems can only really respond to simple tasks under laboratory conditions, and the reported results — organoid training via repeated stimulation, neuron cultures learning predictable patterns through feedback, basic robotic control — are small-scale experiments marking progress from observing brain activity to using biological learning for computation.

The reasonable near-term case for wetware is not that it will displace GPUs. It is that it is an extraordinary instrument for studying human neural tissue — drug screening, disease modeling, and learning research on human cells rather than animal proxies — that also happens to compute.

VIII. Where the Frontiers Meet

Two questions, one shape

The consciousness question in biocomputing is not a fringe concern; it is embedded in the field's founding documents. Creating human brain organoids that can learn, remember, and interact with their environment raises the question of whether they could develop consciousness, even in rudimentary form, and whether they could experience pain or suffering. Bioethicists argue that if biocomputers become conscious, they have moral status, which places limits on permissible research, and researchers note that these concerns will mount as organoids become structurally more complex, receive inputs, generate outputs, and process information about their environment.

Hartung's group adopted an "embedded ethics" approach, partnering with ethicists from the outset so ethical issues are continuously assessed by teams of scientists, ethicists, and the public as the research evolves. The Johns Hopkins Berman Institute has since begun surveying public perception directly.

Note the structural symmetry with the EEG side.

  • EEG asks: what does a neural signal reveal about the person it came from, and what protection is owed to them?
  • Wetware asks: what does a neural tissue become as it grows more complex, and what protection is owed to it?

Both are questions about where the moral line sits relative to a voltage. Neither has an agreed answer. Both are being commercialized now.

The consent chain nobody is watching

There is also a concrete, underexamined link between the two industries: donors.

Organoids are grown from induced pluripotent stem cells derived from ordinary tissue — stem cells derived from human skin, or blood. Someone consented to that donation. The Baltimore Declaration explicitly names the rights of cell donors as an unresolved issue, and the Johns Hopkins ethics literature flags informed consent, moral status, human–animal chimeras, and governance and oversight as open problems requiring a balanced framework.

The question is not abstract. Did a person who donated a skin biopsy for research consent to their cells becoming the computational substrate of a commercial data center in another hemisphere? Do they have any continuing interest in it? If a tissue derived from their cells were ever judged to have moral status, what is their relationship to it? Current consent instruments were not written for this and mostly do not address it.

Meanwhile, on the other side, the neural data of living people is being collected by consumer devices where 96.7% of companies reserve the right to transfer that data to third parties.

The two industries are, in the most literal sense, both running on donated human neural material — one as data, one as tissue — and in both cases the governance is downstream of the deployment.

IX. Conclusion: The Correct Posture

A century after Berger doubted his own oscilloscope, the honest state of the art is more interesting than either the hype or the debunking allows.

On reading brains: EEG remains a synchrony detector with millisecond timing and centimeter localization, physically incapable of resolving distributed asynchronous computation, and it will not be reading your inner monologue — inner speech decoding sits at chance. But foundation models pretrained on tens of thousands of subjects have made state inference far more powerful and far more transferable than it was five years ago; non-invasive text decoding now works when anchored to motor or sensory events; and the scaling curves have not plateaued. The right worry is not mind-reading. It is high-volume, low-accuracy inference applied at scale in employment, insurance, and consumer contexts, by institutions with no obligation to be right.

On computing with neurons: the deployments are real, dated, and institutional — a 20-unit rack of living human neurons commissioned in Singapore in August 2026, backed by an operator with 2.1 GW of conventional bookings. The efficiency claims are directionally grounded in real neurobiology and currently overstated by the life-support overhead. The scaling barriers are physical and unsolved, vascularization foremost. This will not replace silicon in this decade, and the researchers building it say so. It is, however, an important scientific instrument and a genuine ethical frontier.

On both: the legal architecture is being assembled after the fact, in fragments, mostly at the sub-national level, around a definition — "neural data" — that may already be the wrong object, because the harm travels in the inference rather than the signal.

Berger waited five years to publish because he could not believe what he was seeing. The current moment inverts his caution exactly: the instruments are believed far past the point their physics supports, and the tissue is deployed far past the point its ethics are settled. Some of Berger's patience would be useful.
