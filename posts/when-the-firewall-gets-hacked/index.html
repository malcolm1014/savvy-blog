When the Firewall Itself Gets Hacked

Attackers spent at least two weeks inside the servers that run the firewalls at 22 organizations. In the later stages they used a Check Point flaw that had been patched since July. The box that guards your network is now one of the first things attackers go after, which is why it should be the first thing you patch.

By Malcolm R. Smith — Savvy Security LLC · November 2026

The short version

On November 23, 2026, the threat intelligence company GreyNoise reported that an unknown attacker it calls GT13 had taken over 24 Check Point Security Management Servers belonging to at least 22 organizations in 17 countries, across government, finance, energy, transportation, mining, engineering and manufacturing (GreyNoise, 2026).

A management server is the control room for a company’s Check Point firewalls. GreyNoise saw the attacker copy the firewall database and the internal certificate authority, make its own certificate, and at one victim unload the security policies, which GreyNoise says would switch the firewall off.

In the later stages the attacker used CVE-2026-16232, a login bypass that Check Point disclosed and patched in July after it was already being exploited (Rapid7, 2026). How GT13 got in the first time is still unknown. The lesson for everyone else is plain: security gear is a target, so patch it first, keep its admin side off the internet, and check for intruders after you patch.

1 · What happened

GreyNoise runs sensors around the internet that watch who is scanning and attacking what. In this case it got an unusually detailed view of the attacker’s own relay server: more than 1,400 network sessions, 800 command lines and 1,200 files. Its report, written by researcher Steve Miller, lays out the campaign from that vantage point (GreyNoise, 2026).

  • When: GreyNoise confirmed activity from October 17 to November 2, 2026, when its view ended. Its detailed timeline starts earlier, on October 2, with the attacker taking over the relay server. Because the attacker still had persistent access when the view ended, GreyNoise expects the campaign is still going.
  • The relay: The attacker hijacked a file-sharing server running Rejetto HFS through a separate flaw, CVE-2026-61500, and used it as a base to store stolen data and manage victims. It reached victims from 99 IP addresses, mostly ExpressVPN exits and Check Point devices it had already compromised.
  • The work: GreyNoise counted more than 100 management tasks run against Check Point servers through CVE-2026-16232, at least 30 Python scripts, and common tools like Nmap, Chisel and Impacket. On October 26 the attacker sent one bulk certificate task to all 24 servers. Every one of them returned errors, and those error messages included the victims’ addresses, which helped GreyNoise identify many of them.
  • The reach: From the firewalls, the attacker probed deeper: Citrix NetScaler, Ivanti Connect Secure, VMware vCenter, Microsoft Exchange, Oracle databases and Windows domain controllers, including attempts at older, well-known bugs like Netlogon (CVE-2020-1472) and ProxyLogon (CVE-2021-26855).
  • Who: GreyNoise does not attribute GT13 to any known group. It says it suspects the attacker used AI models and agentic tools for scanning, exploitation and tool building, and that some odd stumbles in the timeline look like automated systems getting in each other’s way. That is GreyNoise’s suspicion, worded carefully, not a confirmed finding.

GreyNoise says it is working with government partners and national CERTs to notify the victims. None of the 22 organizations has been named.

2 · The door they used

CVE-2026-16232 is an authentication bypass in the login process for Check Point’s SmartConsole, the program administrators use to manage firewalls. If an attacker can reach the management server’s address over the network, and the server isn’t limited to a list of trusted admin machines, the attacker can get a login token and act as a full administrator without a password (Rapid7, 2026).

  • Disclosed: Check Point published its advisory on July 22, 2026, and said the flaw had already been used against a small number of customers before a fix existed (Rapid7, 2026; Check Point, 2026).
  • Severity: Critical, scored 9.3 under CVSS version 4.
  • Urgency: The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list the same day and gave federal agencies until July 25 to fix it (Rapid7, 2026). Three days.
  • The fix: Jumbo Hotfix R82.10 Take 36, R82 Take 118 and R81.20 Take 158, or later. Rapid7’s summary lists R81.10 and older versions with no fix, so those need an upgrade.
  • The workaround: Restrict SmartConsole’s Trusted Clients to specific admin addresses, never “Any.” Rapid7 notes this narrows who can reach the door but does not remove the bug.

Put those dates next to GreyNoise’s. The patch had been public for about three months when GT13 was using this flaw against two dozen servers. That gap, between “fix available” and “fix installed,” is where most of these stories live.

Read this part twice

Patching closes the door. It does not tell you whether someone walked through it before you closed it. Rapid7’s advice for this flaw is to investigate for compromise even after patching, especially if the management server could be reached from the internet. GT13 copied certificate authorities and made its own certificate. A stolen certificate keeps working after the patch, until someone replaces it.

3 · Why the management server is the crown jewel

Think of a company’s firewalls as the guards at every entrance. The management server is the office that writes their orders. It holds:

  • The rulebook for every firewall. Which traffic is allowed, from where, to which inside systems. Read it and you have a map of the network: what exists, what talks to what, and where the exceptions are.
  • The admin keys. Who can log in and change things.
  • The certificate authority. The internal “passport office” that issues the certificates firewalls use to trust the management server and each other. Steal it and you can print passports the whole system accepts. GreyNoise saw GT13 copy it and create a certificate of its own.
  • The off switch. Whoever controls policy can push new rules, or remove them. At one victim, GT13 unloaded the security policies.

The guard checks everyone’s ID. Nobody checks the guard.

That’s the part that keeps defenders up at night. Most security tools watch laptops and servers. Very few watch the firewall’s own control room, so an attacker who gets in there can sit quietly in the one place built to be trusted.

4 · A pattern, not a fluke

This is not a Check Point problem alone. Attackers have been moving to the edge of the network for years, and the numbers show it.

  • Verizon’s 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found that flaws in VPNs and other edge devices accounted for 22% of breaches that started with a known vulnerability, almost eight times the 3% in the year before (Tenable, 2025).
  • In February 2025, cyber agencies from the US, UK, Canada, Australia and partners published joint guidance on securing edge devices: firewalls, routers, VPN gateways and other internet-facing systems (CISA, 2025).
  • GT13 itself tried bugs in Citrix and Ivanti gear from 2025 and 2024. Same playbook, different logo.

Why attackers like these boxes so much: they sit on the internet by design, they hold the keys to everything behind them, they usually can’t run the security software that would spot an intruder, and patching them means a maintenance window, so it gets pushed to “next month.” Attackers know all four.

5 · Patch the guards first: a checklist

Whether you run Check Point, another brand, or a small office router your internet provider set up, the same habits apply.

  1. Put security gear at the top of the patch list. When a firewall, VPN or management server maker says a flaw is being exploited, treat it like a fire, not a ticket. CISA gave three days for this one.
  2. Keep the admin side off the internet. Management consoles and admin web pages should only be reachable from your admin network or through a VPN. On Check Point, limit SmartConsole’s Trusted Clients to specific addresses, never “Any.”
  3. Patch, then hunt. After installing the fix, look for new admin accounts, unfamiliar login tokens, recent policy changes, new certificates and unknown SSH keys. Check Point (for the July attacks) and GreyNoise (for GT13) have published indicators to search for.
  4. Watch the watcher. Send logs from your security devices to a separate system and alert on the rare, dangerous events: a policy being unloaded, a new administrator, a certificate being issued.
  5. Retire versions that no longer get fixes. If your version is on the “no fix” list, the only fix is an upgrade.
  6. Know what you expose. Check your own public addresses from outside your network every so often. The section below shows how.
  7. Use multi-factor sign-in and named admin accounts. It won’t stop a login bypass, but it stops the stolen-password attacks that usually come next, and named accounts make the logs readable.
  8. If someone else runs your firewall, ask three questions. Is the management side reachable from the internet? When was the last security update installed? Have you checked for signs of compromise since July? A good provider answers in a day.

For the technical: an outside-in check

The simplest test is whether your firewall’s management ports answer from the internet at all. Run this from outside your network, for example a laptop on a phone hotspot, and only against addresses you own. On a Chromebook, it runs in the Linux (Crostini) terminal.

# Install nmap once
sudo apt install nmap

# Replace with YOUR office's public IP address
nmap -Pn -p 443,18190,18264,18265,19009 203.0.113.10

PORT      STATE    SERVICE
443/tcp   filtered https
18190/tcp filtered unknown
19009/tcp filtered unknown
18264/tcp open     unknown   ← answers from the internet: find out why

“Filtered” or “closed” is what you want. Ports 18190 and 19009 are the ones SmartConsole uses to reach a Check Point management server, and 18264 and 18265 are certificate services, per Check Point’s community port table. Check the official port list for your version, and remember a site-to-site VPN may need some of these open between your own sites, not to the whole world. Patch the guards first. Then check that the guards are still yours.

If you run a small business and someone else handles your network, forward this to them with the three questions from the checklist. If you run the network yourself, the hour it takes to check the management side is cheaper than finding out the way 22 organizations just did.
