The AI That Checks Every Door

The European Commission says attackers misused a publicly available AI tool against it. The tool doesn’t need secret exploits. It needs the doors you forgot were open.

By Malcolm R. Smith — Savvy Security LLC · November 2026

1 · What happened 2 · The tool 3 · Why the math changed 4 · Lock down first For the technical Still open Sources

The short version

This week the European Commission confirmed that a cyber campaign against it was carried out with an AI tool. Its spokesperson called it “a misuse of an AI tool” and said “it was an AI tool that is publicly available” (Help Net Security, 2026). The Commission took several internal applications offline to protect them while it dealt with the attack.

Reporting points to ARTEX, an open-source framework built for legitimate penetration testing, which the EU’s own cyber response team had warned about two weeks earlier. The Commission has not confirmed the name.

You are not the European Commission. That is the point. A tool like this costs attackers almost nothing to point at you, and it is very good at finding the small, boring mistakes that most small offices have. The fixes are boring too, and they are at the end.

1 · What happened

According to the Commission, the attack was detected on October 22, 2026 and confirmed after an initial investigation on October 26. As a protective step it “temporarily took down several applications,” and “most of them have since then been restored” (Help Net Security, 2026).

The public did not hear about it until Politico reported it this week. At the Commission’s midday briefing on Thursday, spokesperson Thomas Regnier confirmed it. He would not say who was behind it (“on the attribution, I will not go there”), would not name the services that were hit, and called it “a campaign,” meaning “a series of incidents” rather than one break-in (Help Net Security, 2026).

Two things he did say are worth holding onto. First: “we have not detected any evidence of a data breach affecting Commission staff as a whole,” with the investigation still going. Second, there was no ransom note: “I’m really not aware of any ransomware request made to the Commission” (Help Net Security, 2026).

How did they get in?

Nobody has said. The Commission has not described the entry point, and none of the reporting I read names a phishing email, a specific vulnerability or an exposed service. Be careful with any article that tells you exactly how it happened. Right now, nobody outside the investigation knows.

2 · The tool

Politico reported that CERT-EU, the cyber response team for EU institutions, saw the attackers using ARTEX (Help Net Security, 2026). On November 5, CERT-EU had already published a warning about it, calling it “an AI-powered penetration-testing framework” (CERT-EU, 2026).

Penetration testing is the legal, paid version of hacking: a company hires testers to break in before criminals do. ARTEX automates that job. It does not come with an AI of its own. The person running it connects it to a commercial AI model of their choice, and the model decides what to try next while the framework runs the scanning and testing tools a human tester would use (CERT-EU, 2026). That is the “rent” in this story: the framework is free, and the brains are rented by the hour from an AI provider.

The original project was taken down in October after reports of misuse, but copies of it, called forks, are still online (Help Net Security, 2026). CERT-EU says it has seen similar AI-driven activity against the bodies it protects, “highly likely involving ARTEX,” and warned that “ARTEX’s public availability will likely accelerate adoption by threat actors” (Help Net Security, 2026).

The framework is free. The brains are rented by the hour. Your forgotten router login is the door.

3 · Why the math changed

For years, small businesses have been protected by a quiet bit of economics. A skilled attacker’s time is expensive, so they spend it on targets worth the effort. A dental office or a three-person accounting firm usually wasn’t.

Automation breaks that. When software does the patient part of the work (find what’s exposed, try the obvious logins, look for an old version, try again), the cost of checking one more business drops close to zero. An attacker can point it at thousands of addresses and only look at the ones that come back interesting.

The part of CERT-EU’s warning that matters most for a small office is this: AI tooling can chain “minor misconfigurations into higher-impact attack paths” (CERT-EU, 2026). A router admin page that faces the internet is a small problem. An old password on it is a small problem. A file share that trusts anything on the inside network is a small problem. Strung together by something tireless, they are a break-in.

No secret exploits required. Most coverage of ARTEX agrees on one thing: it succeeds mainly by finding ordinary weaknesses, not by using undiscovered “zero-day” flaws. That is bad news and good news. Bad, because ordinary weaknesses are everywhere. Good, because you already know how to fix them.

4 · What to patch and lock down first

You don’t need an AI of your own to defend against this. You need to close the doors it checks. In order of what I’d do this weekend:

  1. List everything you have on the internet. Website, email, the router, any remote desktop, a camera system, a network storage box, a booking plugin, that test site from two years ago. CERT-EU’s first piece of advice is to limit internet exposure to only what needs it (CERT-EU, 2026). You can’t limit what you haven’t listed.
  2. Take admin pages off the internet. Router settings, camera admin, server consoles and remote desktop should not answer to the whole world. Reach them over a VPN or from inside the office only. CERT-EU specifically says to keep management and control panels off the internet (CERT-EU, 2026).
  3. Patch the outside first, and fast. Anything that faces the internet gets updated before anything else. CERT-EU’s wording is to patch internet-exposed assets on an emergency basis (CERT-EU, 2026). Turn on automatic updates for your router, website plugins and storage box if they offer it.
  4. Fix the small stuff too. The warnings you’ve been ignoring because they’re “low” are exactly what this kind of tool strings together. CERT-EU tells defenders to keep minor findings in the fix-it queue (CERT-EU, 2026).
  5. Turn on multi-factor sign-in everywhere it counts. Email first, then every admin login and every remote access tool. Look for the one account that never got it, usually an old shared login or a former employee.
  6. Change old passwords and keys. Default passwords on devices, passwords shared with a past contractor, and any key or password that has ever been pasted into a website’s code or a public code repository.
  7. Check which apps have access to your accounts. In Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, look at the third-party apps that were granted access to mail and files. Remove anything nobody recognizes.
  8. Keep logs, and look at them once a week. Turn on sign-in alerts for email and admin accounts. Automated attacks are noisy: lots of failed logins and scanning from strange places. Somebody should notice.

For the technical: see what the internet sees

These commands show what your office looks like from the outside. They work on Linux and macOS; on a Chromebook, run them in the Linux (Crostini) terminal after sudo apt install nmap curl. Only scan addresses you own or are allowed to test. Run the scan from outside your office network (a phone hotspot works), or your router will answer for the inside.

# 1. From inside the office: what is our public address?
curl -s https://ifconfig.me
203.0.113.27   ← example address

# 2. From OUTSIDE the office: which common doors answer?
nmap -Pn --top-ports 100 203.0.113.27
443/tcp  open   https
3389/tcp open   ms-wbt-server   ← remote desktop open to the world
8080/tcp open   http-proxy      ← often a router or camera admin page

# 3. Anything you didn't expect? Find out what it is before you close it.
nmap -Pn -sV -p 3389,8080 203.0.113.27

If anything besides your website or your VPN answers, it belongs on your list from step 1. Closing it is usually a setting in the router called port forwarding.
