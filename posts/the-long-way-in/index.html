The Long Way In

How a one-man research shop in Hernando County ended up at the table with a federal research program — and what it actually took.

By Malcolm Smith — Savvy Security LLC · August 2026

The version of this story that usually gets told online is a lie of omission. Somebody posts the win and skips the fourteen months. There's no secret here — there's a sequence: a paper nobody asked for, a reviewer who told me the presentation was a mess, an entity registration, a federal program that lapsed out from under me for seven months, one confidently fabricated funding opportunity I almost trusted, and a university partner who said yes. Here it is straight, including the parts that went sideways.

I want to tell this straight, because the version of this story that gets told on the internet is usually a lie of omission. Somebody posts the win and skips the fourteen months. Then everybody else reads it, decides they must be missing some secret, and quits.

There's no secret. There's a sequence. Here's mine, including the parts that went sideways.

It started with a paper nobody asked for

Early this year I wrote a white paper. Not for a client, not for a grant, not for anybody — I wrote it because I'd been chewing on the same problem for years and I finally wanted it on paper properly.

The problem, stated as broadly as I'm willing to state it: modern attacks move faster than humans can respond, and the response has to happen down at the kernel level, in the first milliseconds, in environments that have no cloud to phone home to. Machine-speed defense for places where there is nobody to call. That's the whole thesis. The architecture behind it is the company's intellectual property and it stays in the vault.

I put it out into the world with roughly zero expectations.

Three weeks later I had a meeting request from the CEO and the co-founder of a security startup in Paris.

That was the first thing that told me the work was real. Not a customer, not money — just two serious people spending an hour of their time because something I wrote was worth an hour of their time. If you take one thing from this article: published work is the cheapest credential there is, and it does not care where you live.

The part where somebody good tells you it isn't good enough

By spring I had a body of research — the core architecture plus a stack of supporting papers — and I sent it out to a review panel of senior people in the field. People whose names would make you sit up.

One of them came back and told me, in effect, that the work was strong and the presentation was a mess. That a reviewer opening it cold got nine trees and no forest. That I'd written for people who already understood it.

That stung for about a day. Then I rebuilt the whole front end of the research program around plain-language framing — what this is, who it's for, and exactly what I'm asking each reviewer to evaluate.

That single piece of criticism was worth more than every compliment I got that month. When a serious person tells you what's wrong, they are spending their own credibility to help you. Take it.

Then the boring part, which is the actual moat

Here's where most people fall off. The paperwork.

May 7 — Savvy Security LLC registered with the Florida Division of Corporations. Sunbiz, $125, manager-managed. That's the day the company legally existed.

June 17 — SAM.gov registration went active. That's the federal portal. No SAM registration, no federal award, full stop. It took weeks, it involved a login.gov account, an entity validation process, an IRS taxpayer match, and about four hundred yes/no questions about FAR and DFARS clauses that I had never heard of in my life.

Two things I got wrong that I'll save you:

  • It's SAM.gov. Not SAMS. I lost time on that.
  • You do not apply for a CAGE code. The Defense Logistics Agency assigns you exactly one, automatically, at the end of SAM registration. What you do choose are your NAICS and PSC codes — those describe what you actually sell, and they matter enormously for what opportunities find you.

None of this is glamorous. All of it is load-bearing. A brilliant technology with a lapsed SAM registration is a brilliant technology that cannot be paid.

The wave that nearly wiped the whole thing out

The federal SBIR and STTR programs — the small-business research funding pipeline this entire plan depended on — lapsed in September 2025. Gone. Unauthorized.

They came back on April 13, 2026, when the Small Business Innovation and Economic Security Act was signed, reauthorizing both through 2031.

I want to be honest about what that means: I was building toward a door that, for part of the time I was building, did not exist. If the reauthorization hadn't passed, this article would be about something else entirely. Some of what happens to you in this game is weather. You can't control the weather. You can only be the person who is ready when it clears.

The mistake I want you to learn from more than any other

At one point earlier this year, I was working through funding strategy with AI assistance and came away with a specific program name, a specific funding tier, and a specific dollar figure — delivered with total confidence.

Months later I went back and checked it against the agency's own published topic list.

None of it was real. The program name didn't exist. The funding tier didn't exist. It had been generated, fluently and convincingly, out of nothing.

I use AI heavily. I build with it every day. It is the single biggest force multiplier a one-person shop has ever had. And it will hand you a fabricated federal funding opportunity with a straight face and a sense of urgency.

Verify everything against the primary source. The agency's own site. The actual solicitation. The contracting officer. If a number matters — if you'd put it in a proposal, a pitch, or a Facebook post — it needs a government URL behind it, not a chat window.

That check cost me an afternoon. Not checking would have cost me my reputation with the first program manager who noticed.

Finding the partner

Here's the piece of structure that changed everything for me.

There are two flavors of this funding. SBIR is the small business alone. STTR requires a formal partnership with a research institution — a university — where the company does at least 40% of the work and the university does at least 30%.

Most people read that as a constraint. It's the opposite. It's a reason for a university to take your call. You are not asking a professor for a favor. You are arriving with a funded research scope, a co-Principal-Investigator seat, work for graduate students, and publishable results.

So I approached the University of South Florida and its Institute of Applied Engineering. I explained what the university side of the work would be — measurement and validation science, real numbers on real hardware, results their lab could publish under its own name.

Then I waited. And I hedged, because you always hedge — I opened a parallel conversation with a cyber-focused university in another state so that "no" from one wouldn't be "no" from everyone.

I also found out there's a Florida program, the High Tech Corridor Matching Grants Research Program, that will match an industry partner's investment in university research with up to $150,000 — paid to the faculty team, not to me. Which means what I was actually offering USF was roughly double what my own budget line looked like.

And I brought in an APEX Accelerator counselor. These are free, federally funded, in every state, and almost nobody uses them. Mine checks my registrations, walks me through compliance requirements, and gives me neutral advice with nothing to sell me.

In August, USF and IAE faculty said yes.

Where it stands today

The proposal is secured. The research partner is secured. We're moving on a Direct-to-Phase-II track — the path you take when you already have a working prototype and can skip the feasibility phase. The current Department of Defense solicitation window opened August 26 and closes September 23.

Nothing is awarded. I want to say that plainly, because I've watched people announce awards they hadn't won and I've watched what it cost them. The proposal is in motion and the partner is real. That's the milestone. The rest is a decision somebody else makes on a timeline I don't control.

But fourteen months ago I was a guy in Spring Hill with an idea and a text editor.

The sequence, if you want to run it

  1. Publish something. Real work, under your name, where people can find it. This is the whole engine. Nothing else on this list works without it.
  2. Register the entity. Your state's corporations division. In Florida that's Sunbiz. It's a few hundred dollars and an afternoon.
  3. Get an EIN from the IRS. Free, ten minutes, online.
  4. Register on SAM.gov. Free. Takes weeks. Start it before you think you need it. Your CAGE code comes automatically at the end.
  5. Pick your NAICS and PSC codes carefully. This is how the government finds you.
  6. Call your APEX Accelerator. Free counseling, every state. Do this early, not late.
  7. Read the actual solicitations at sbir.gov and your target agency's own portal. Not summaries. Not somebody's blog. Not an AI's recollection.
  8. If your work has a research component, go STTR and bring a university a real offer, not a request.
  9. Find your end user. A letter from someone inside the agency who wants the thing you're building is worth more than another paragraph of technical description.
  10. Renew your SAM registration annually. Set the calendar reminder the day you activate. A lapsed registration disqualifies you mid-contract.

What I'd tell you if we were sitting across a table

You do not need permission. You need a body of work, a legal entity, a federal registration, and the patience to sit in a queue.

The gatekeepers you're imagining mostly aren't there. What's actually there is a wall of tedious, unglamorous process that filters out almost everybody — not because it's hard, but because it's boring and slow and nobody's cheering while you do it.

I'm not smarter than the people reading this. I've been at this twenty years, I know how to find things, and I was too stubborn to stop when the program I was betting on got un-funded for seven months.

If any of you want to walk this path, my inbox is open. I'll tell you what I got wrong so you can skip it.

Go build something.

There's no secret. There's a sequence — including the parts that went sideways.
