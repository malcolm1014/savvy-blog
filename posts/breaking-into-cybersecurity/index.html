Everybody Lied to You About Breaking Into Cybersecurity

The real shortage isn't at the entry level. It's trust — and there's a map for closing that gap.

By Malcolm R. Smith — Savvy Security LLC

The "4.8 million unfilled cybersecurity jobs" flyer isn't lying, exactly — it's just pointing at the wrong door. Only 4% of employers say entry-level roles are hard to fill; 72% of the real pain is mid-level and up. So why do 200 applications get you silence? Because the industry doesn't have a résumé problem. It has a trust problem — and trust is built in a room, not a job board. This piece walks through the data, the fix, and a free map of 5,000+ rooms to go stand in.

You've seen the post. "4.8 MILLION unfilled cybersecurity jobs!" Grab a cert, print a résumé, back the truck up to the money pile.

Then you apply to 200 jobs and hear nothing. Not a rejection. Nothing.

So let's talk about what's actually going on — because the real answer is more encouraging than the fake one.

The numbers, honestly

The demand is real. U.S. employers posted 514,359 cybersecurity job openings in the 12 months ending March 2026 — up 12% from the year before. Cybersecurity is the only major tech sector still hiring above its pre-pandemic level, while software development and data analytics both sit below theirs.

But here's the part nobody prints on the flyer.

SANS looked at where hiring is actually hard in 2026. Seventy-two percent of the difficulty is concentrated in mid-level, senior, and expert roles. Only four percent of companies say they struggle to fill entry-level seats.

Read that again. The shortage isn't at the front door.

Meanwhile, ISC2's workforce study finds 31% of security teams have zero entry-level staff, and it's an open secret that plenty of employers list "entry-level" requisitions demanding certifications that themselves require years of field experience to even sit for.

So the problem was never a shortage of chairs.

It's a shortage of trust. Employers aren't drowning in résumés they can't read. They're short on people they can vouch for.

So how do strangers become trusted?

Look at how hiring actually works.

Referrals make up roughly 7% of the applicant pile — and somewhere between 30% and 50% of the people who actually get hired. A referred candidate's odds of landing an interview run several times higher than a cold applicant's, and referred hires stick around longer once they're in.

(You'll also see people claim "80% of jobs are never posted." That number is shaky and economists argue about it — I'm not going to sell it to you. The referral math above is solid on its own, and it's more than enough to make the point.)

A referral moves fast. A public job posting sits open for weeks while a manager quietly asks their own team first. You don't beat that system by applying harder. You beat it by being someone's answer when they get asked.

Which is why I built a map

Here's the thing that took me way too long to figure out: the cybersecurity community isn't a walled garden. It's a physical, in-person, mostly free thing that meets monthly, probably within driving distance of you right now.

Hacker cons. DEF CON Groups. 2600 meetings. OWASP chapters. Hackerspaces. Ham radio clubs. Library makerspaces. Cyber schools. Youth STEM programs. Government hubs. Certification testing sites.

Most people never go because they never knew it existed.

How to actually use it

Don't just browse it and close the tab. Run these plays.

1. Hit "Near me"

First click. Write down the three closest spots to you, whatever they are. Visit all three in the next 60 days. Don't agonize over which is the "right" one — the goal is contact, not optimization.

2. Open "Upcoming" and grab a date

Find the first event within 60 days and 90 minutes of you. Put it in your calendar. One confirmed date beats an infinite list of maybes.

3. Find what recurs

Filter to DEF CON Groups and 2600 meetings — these happen every month. Pick one and go three months in a row. First visit you're a stranger. Second, you're a face. Third, you're a regular. That third visit is where careers start.

4. Filter by what you actually like

Use the Topics filter on what genuinely interests you — not what you think looks employable. Enthusiasm is memorable. Box-checking isn't.

5. Find your exam site early

Use the Certification filter to find your testing center before you're ready. It makes the goal real, and study groups tend to cluster nearby.

6. Thinking about moving?

Filter by state and count. A city with a BSides, two DEF CON Groups, an OWASP chapter, and a cyber school has visible doors. A city with none is a job market you'd be walking into blind.

7. Got kids?

Filter to youth STEM and library makerspaces. Those programs are also always short on volunteer mentors — and volunteering puts you shoulder to shoulder with working professionals.

One heads-up: locations are approximate and grassroots groups move around. Check the group's own page before you drive.

Three rules so you don't burn yourself

Never touch the network. Don't scan, sniff, or "test" anything at a con or meetup. It's the fastest way to get permanently uninvited from everything.

Don't ask "how do I get into cyber?" It's the most-asked, least-answerable question in the field. Ask this instead: "I finished the TryHackMe SOC path and built a Wazuh lab. I'm targeting SOC analyst roles. What gap would you notice first on my résumé?" Specific questions get generous answers.

Bring something. Snacks, a ride, help hauling gear, a summary of a talk. The fastest route from stranger to regular is being useful.

Open the map. Hit "Near me." Put one thing on your calendar.

Then go make a free TryHackMe account and finish one room tonight.
