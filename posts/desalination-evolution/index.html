The Long Ascent of Sweet Water

Five thousand years of desalination, civilization by civilization — from a sponge over a brass cauldron to a steam-driven membrane grid that supplies four-fifths of a nation's tap water.

Abstract

Desalination is often narrated as a modern engineering achievement with a decorative classical prologue. The documentary record supports a different reading: the principle was settled in the fourth century BCE, the apparatus matured across a millennium of Greek, Chinese, Roman and Islamic practice, and the economics remained prohibitive until two twentieth-century inventions — multi-stage flash distillation (patented 1957) and the asymmetric cellulose-acetate membrane (1959) — decoupled fresh water from both rainfall and heat.

This review reconstructs that sequence contribution by contribution, distinguishing what the sources establish from what they infer. It closes on the State of Israel, whose six coastal reverse-osmosis plants now deliver roughly 800 million cubic metres per year — 70–80% of national potable demand — and which in November 2025 became the first country to pump desalinated seawater into a natural freshwater lake.

Two cautions frame the whole: the ancient record is thin and partly inferential, and the modern record is contested even in its headline numbers. Both are flagged in place rather than smoothed over.

Five findings
  1. Theory preceded hardware by ~500 years. Aristotle stated the phase-change law c. 340 BCE; the earliest depicted apparatus is Alexander of Aphrodisias, c. 200 CE.
  2. China's contribution was non-thermal. Warring-States texts describe salt adsorption on bamboo mats — a sorption idea, not a distillation one — plus documented freeze separation using sea ice.
  3. Industrialisation supplied the boiler, not the motive. Steam-plant demand for pure feedwater, not thirst, drove nineteenth-century evaporator design.
  4. The real discontinuity is energy, not scale. Seawater RO fell from ~16 kWh/m³ in the 1970s to under 2 kWh/m³ today, against a thermodynamic floor near 1.
  5. Israel's achievement is systemic. Not one plant but seven, cross-linked to a reversible national carrier — an insurance architecture, not a production record.

I. The ledger of contributions

Who added what, and when — with the evidentiary standing of each claim stated plainly.

ca. 3200–1100 BCE
Minoan Crete & the Bronze-Age Aegean
Inferred

The earliest layer of the story is also the weakest. Angelakis and colleagues, in the most comprehensive scholarly survey of desalination's history, write that Minoan sailors "probably implemented desalination for thousands of years by boiling water to evaporate and separate freshwater from the salt" — a reasonable inference from Minoan maritime range and cookware, not a documented practice. What is archaeologically attested is adjacent and arguably more impressive: a clay oblong water filter operated under turbulent flow so that its porous surface self-cleaned, which the same authors call "a precursor of the ceramic membranes."

Contribution: potable-water treatment as routine infrastructure aboard ships and in palaces; the filter-as-membrane concept. Not established: any dated Minoan distillation apparatus. Claims of "documented boiling by 3200 BCE" overstate the record.

ca. 610–322 BCE
Ionian & Classical Greece
Textually documented

This is the era's decisive contribution, and it is intellectual rather than mechanical. Anaximander (c. 610–547 BCE) framed water as a recycling meteorological system. Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BCE) contributed the cloth bag filter still called the "Hippocratic sleeve." Then Aristotle, in Meteorologica, states the law on which every thermal desalination plant since has run: "Saltwater, when it turns into vapour, becomes sweet, and the vapour does not form saltwater again when it condenses" — and, pointedly, adds "this I identify by experimentation." Britannica dates the associated sailors' evaporation practice to the fourth century BCE.

Aristotle also reports a wax vessel sealed and submerged in the sea yielding drinkable water — an early, and physically dubious, claim of separation by material rather than by heat. Contribution: the phase-change principle, stated as reproducible experiment; the first articulation of desalination as a method rather than an accident.

ca. 475 BCE – 1279 CE
Warring States, Han & Song China
Textually documented

China's line of development is independent of the Greek one and conceptually distinct. The Classic of Mountains and Seas (Warring States, c. 475–221 BCE) and Eastern Han texts record that bamboo mats used repeatedly for steaming rice developed a thin surface film with adsorption and ion-exchange behaviour capable of taking up salt. Lei's Treatise on Preparing Drugs (Southern Song) records steamer-and-mat desalting of kelp. A Song-era account of a "sea well" lowered into the ocean to draw fresh water is legend, but signals that the goal was current among Tang and Song mariners.

Separately, farmers on China's northern coast used sea ice — nearly fresh, because salt is excluded during crystal growth — to maintain soil moisture: the first recorded direct use of freeze desalination. Contribution: sorption and freeze separation, two of the four modern families, roughly two millennia before either was industrialised.

1st – 3rd century CE
Rome & the Hellenistic Mediterranean
Textually documented

Roman practice was filtration-first: clay filters to trap salt, sand and gravel beds, and a wool wick siphon in which salts were retained by the fibre. Pliny the Elder describes crews spreading fleeces on deck overnight and wringing out condensed sea mist — condensation harvesting, not distillation, and worth distinguishing.

The pivotal document belongs to Alexander of Aphrodisias, c. 200 CE, who describes and illustrates seamen boiling saltwater in a brass vessel with a large sponge suspended over the mouth to absorb the vapour, then drinking the water squeezed from the sponge. This — not Aristotle — is the first written and depicted indication of desalination by boiling. Contribution: the earliest recorded working apparatus, plus the alembic-adjacent glassware of Hellenistic Alexandria that the next era would inherit.

7th – 14th century
The Islamic scientific world
Textually documented

Where antiquity supplied the principle, the Islamic world supplied the instrument. Britannica records that "an Arab writer of the 8th century CE produced a treatise on distillation"; Angelakis et al. identify the best-known figure as Jabir ibn Hayyan ("Geber"), whose distillation writings date to the late seventh century, with later contributions from Abu Mansur al-Harawi. The refined alembic — controlled vessel, condensing head, receiver — is the direct ancestor of every still that followed, and this knowledge, layered on Greek, Persian and Egyptian sources, reached Western Europe through the Iberian Peninsula.

Contribution: distillation as a documented, transmissible technology with purpose-built apparatus and a written literature — the step that made later European work cumulative rather than repetitive.

1452 – 1800
Renaissance & early-modern Europe
Documented

A long plateau punctuated by good ideas that would not scale. Leonardo da Vinci proposed producing distilled water cheaply by adapting a still to a cookstove — the first explicit attempt at cost engineering. In 1560, a besieged garrison on an island off Tunisia reportedly built a still for survival. Francis Bacon tried sand filtration in 1627; Sir Richard Hawkins reported supplying his crew by shipboard distillation in 1662; the first desalination patents went to William Walcot (1675) and Robert Fitzgerald and others (1683). None entered service: scaling defeated them all. In the new United States, Thomas Jefferson compiled the workable heat-based methods and had instructions printed on the backs of ships' clearance permits — an early act of state technology transfer.

Contribution: intellectual property, publication and distribution of method. No measurable improvement in the basic process between the mid-1600s and 1800.

1800 – 1945
Industrial Britain, empire & the first plants
Documented / contested

The steam engine changed the incentive. Boilers required pure feedwater, thermodynamics gave engineers a theory of heat reuse, and colonial expansion put garrisons on arid coasts. Multiple-effect evaporators let one unit of heat work through several stages; in 1852 Alphonse Normandy patented a vertical-tube seawater still that became the maritime standard because it was simple to build. The US Army installed Normandy evaporators at Key West and Dry Tortugas in the 1860s at about 26 m³/day each; a six-effect plant at Suakin in the 1880s made roughly 350 tonnes/day for British troops.

The commonly cited "first commercial plant" — Tigné, Sliema, Malta, 1881 — deserves care. IWA's review notes that "vague and contradictory information often makes it impossible to pinpoint" such milestones, that many scholars accept Malta, and that comparable works existed on the Pacific coast of Peru, Bolivia and Chile at the same date. Maltese records add a deflating coda: the machinery was so costly to build and run that it was abandoned by 1885.

Land-based continuity arrives with Curaçao, 1928 — a 60 m³/day multiple-effect plant, modest in output but permanent in kind. Contribution: heat recovery, mass manufacture, and the shift from survival device to municipal asset.

1945 – 1980
The state-science era: MSF and the Gulf
Documented

Desalination became an object of national policy. The Saline Water Act (1952) and the Office of Saline Water (1955) funded American membrane and thermal research; President Kennedy called the work "more important than any other scientific enterprise in which the USA is now engaged." Freeport, Texas, opened the first US industrial plant in 1961 after prolonged drought.

The decisive thermal invention was multi-stage flash, patented by Professor R. S. Silver in 1957: seawater is flashed into steam across a cascade of successively lower-pressure chambers, each stage reusing the previous stage's latent heat. Kuwait commissioned four MSF units of 2,271 m³/day in 1957, and the first major commercial plants — two units of 4,550 m³/day for Kuwait and one of 2,775 m³/day for Guernsey — came on stream in 1960. MSF displaced multiple-effect evaporation and, coupled with power-station steam in Gulf co-generation plants, produced the first megaprojects.

Contribution: Gulf states, Britain and the United States jointly converted desalination into utility-scale industry — at a thermal price of roughly 20–27 kWh/m³ that only cheap hydrocarbons could absorb.

1959 – present
The membrane revolution
Primary sources

Reverse osmosis removes the phase change altogether: pressure alone drives water through a semi-permeable film while salts are rejected. Two American laboratory results made it real. Reid and Breton (1959) found that cellulose acetate films rejected salt at usable ratios, though fluxes were too low to matter. Then at UCLA, Sidney Loeb and Srinivasa Sourirajan cast the asymmetric membrane — a dense skin under a tenth of a micron thick on a porous support — which raised flux by orders of magnitude. Loeb's own account dates the breakthrough casting work to 1959; the canonical publication is 1963, which is why sources differ on the date.

Packaging followed physics: General Atomics introduced the spiral-wound module in 1963, still the industry standard; Loeb and McCutchan piloted the technology at Coalinga, California, the first municipal utility to run RO, in 1965; the first seawater RO plant followed in 1975. Thin-film composite aromatic-polyamide membranes cut specific energy sharply from the late 1970s.

The second half of the efficiency story is energy recovery from the brine stream: Francis turbines (75–80% recovery), Pelton wheels (80–85%), hydraulic turbochargers in the 1990s (~90%), and finally positive-displacement isobaric energy-recovery devices reaching up to 98%, standard in efficient plants since about 2005–06. Contribution: a physical process in place of a thermal one — and the fall from ~16 kWh/m³ to under 2.

1964 – 2026
Israel: from scarcity to engineered surplus
Operator & regulator data

Israel's distinctive contribution is not a membrane or a pump. It is the decision to treat manufactured water as the base load of a national grid, and to build the grid to match. The National Water Carrier of the 1960s moved northern water south; the desalination programme of the 2000s inverted the logic, putting production on the Mediterranean coast beside demand and using the carrier for redistribution.

The build-out is legible as a sequence: Ashkelon (2005, 120 MCM/yr), Palmachim (2007, 90), Hadera (2010, 145), Sorek (2013, 150), Ashdod (2015, 100), and Sorek 2 / Be'er Miriam (full operation 2025, 200). Sorek 1 set two benchmarks at once: 16-inch membrane elements mounted vertically to shrink the footprint, IDE's pressure-centre design for efficiency, and a then-record low water price in a build-operate-transfer tender. Sorek 2 is the world's first steam-driven SWRO plant, adds carbon capture and on-site chemical production, and again claims a world-record low cost.

Two 2025–26 developments matter more than capacity. First, the seventh plant — Western Galilee / Birkat Miriam, €425 million with €150 million from the European Investment Bank — is sited in the north, ending the coastal-centre concentration; it is expected online around 2027 with 100 MCM/yr. Second, in November 2025 Israel began pumping desalinated water into the Sea of Galilee through a reversed carrier, after the lake fell below its lower red line at −213 m. Mekorot's stated long-term target for that reverse flow is 15,000 m³/hour.

The forward plan is explicit: a government-approved 50-year framework targeting 2.3 billion m³/yr of desalinated water by 2050 and 2.75 billion by 2075, opening large-plant development to private builders at a minimum scale of 100 MCM/yr. Contribution: desalination as redundant, reversible national infrastructure — and the first use of manufactured water to recharge a natural reservoir.

II. Where the evidence pushes back

A review that only ascends is not a review. Four counterweights sit in the same literature as the achievements.

The headline capacity number is contested. IDRA reports roughly 91.5 million m³/day installed as of 2024, with about 105 million committed. The European Commission's Blue Economy Observatory, on the same page, cites "approximately 135 million m³/day" operating across more than 22,000 plants. Wikipedia carries ~97 million for 2020 and ~99 million for 2023. These are not reconcilable by rounding; they reflect different definitions of installed, contracted and operating capacity. Any figure quoted without its definition should be treated as unreliable.

Brine now exceeds product. Global assessments put brine discharge above the volume of fresh water produced — on the order of 142–150 million m³/day. In the Persian Gulf, a shallow semi-enclosed basin averaging 35 m deep, cumulative discharge has left the water roughly 25% saltier than open seawater, raising the prospect of a "peak salt" ceiling on the same coastline that pioneered MSF.

The energy is still mostly fossil. Over 95% of Middle Eastern desalination energy came from fossil fuels as of recent assessment, and in 2023 desalination consumed nearly half of the region's residential-sector energy. Efficiency gains are real; decarbonisation is not yet.

Product water is not neutral. Reverse osmosis strips iodine. Israeli researchers found 62% of school-age children and 85% of pregnant women below the WHO adequacy range, in a country with no universal salt-iodisation programme and heavy SWRO reliance. The public-health cost of a water-security success is a genuine finding, not a footnote.

“By 3200 BCE, evidence suggests Minoan sailors utilized rudimentary boiling techniques.”
Angelakis et al. write that Minoans probably did so; no dated apparatus or text exists. Attested Minoan technology is ceramic filtration.

Aristotle documented the “Sponge Method” — sponges over boiling brass cauldrons.
Aristotle stated the phase-change principle. The vessel-and-sponge description and illustration are Alexander of Aphrodisias, c. 200 CE — some five centuries later.

Correct in substance; the specific attribution is R. S. Silver's 1957 patent, with first commercial plants in 1960 (Kuwait, Guernsey).

Directionally right, imprecisely stated. Water Authority figures give 70–80% of potable demand from six plants; EIB projects 85–90% of household and industrial consumption after the seventh. Fanack, citing 2023 data, gives 50% of potable water.
IMPRECISE

IV. Conclusion

Read across five millennia, the curve in Figure 1 is not a story of gradually better boiling. It is three discontinuities separated by long plateaus. The Greeks converted an observation into a law. Islamic chemistry converted the law into an instrument and a literature. And the twentieth century converted the instrument into an economy — first by recycling heat across stages, then by abandoning heat altogether.

Israel's role is the fourth kind of contribution, and the one least visible in a capacity chart: institutional. Seven plants, a reversible carrier, tendered build-operate-transfer contracts, private-development rules with a 100 MCM/yr floor, and a fifty-year production target amount to treating water like an electricity grid — dispatchable, redundant, and planned against demand curves rather than rainfall.

The open problems are correspondingly institutional. Brine volumes now exceed product volumes. Regional energy remains overwhelmingly fossil-fuelled. Product water is nutritionally incomplete. On present evidence the next real advance will not be a better membrane — the thermodynamic headroom is nearly gone — but a plant whose outputs are all accounted for.
