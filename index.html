The Physics of Transmission: Why Not All Light Is Created Equal for Data

By Malcolm R. Smith — Principal Manager, Savvy Security LLC

When we talk about transmitting data—whether across a Wi-Fi link, down an undersea cable, or through deep space—we are fundamentally talking about modulating electromagnetic waves. But a common question in systems architecture is: does the specific spectrum or wavelength of light change the information density and transmission performance?

The short answer is yes. The physics of wave propagation, quantum photonics, and material optics dictate that different wavelengths excel at drastically different tasks.

1. Frequency Dictates Bandwidth (The Shannon-Hartley Limit)

At the foundation of information theory is the Shannon-Hartley Theorem, which proves that the maximum theoretical data throughput of any channel is directly proportional to its available bandwidth (B).

Capacity (C) = B · log2( 1 + S/N )

Because electromagnetic frequency (ν) increases as wavelength (λ) shortens (c = λν), higher-frequency spectrum regions contain vast operational bandwidths compared to lower-frequency radio waves:

  • 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi: a 1% operational bandwidth slice gives you roughly 24 MHz of usable spectrum.
  • 1550 nm Near-Infrared Laser: a 1% operational bandwidth slice gives you 1.93 THz of usable spectrum.

Higher frequencies allow us to package exponentially more bits per second into a carrier signal — the core reason why optical laser communication offers 10x to 100x the data throughput of traditional RF for deep-space communications.

2. The Medium Dictates the Winner (Attenuation Windows)

Having ultra-high potential bandwidth means nothing if the signal cannot pass through the physical medium without degrading. The choice of wavelength is usually dictated by attenuation (scattering and absorption):

  • Fiber Optics (1550 nm): standard silica glass fiber exhibits a global attenuation minimum (~0.2 dB/km) around 1550 nm (the C-Band). Shorter wavelengths suffer from Rayleigh scattering (∝ 1/λ⁴), while longer wavelengths hit the physical absorption limits of silica lattice vibrations.
  • Subsea Communications (450–550 nm): water absorbs infrared and radio waves almost instantaneously. However, clear seawater exhibits a tight optical transmission window in the blue-green spectrum, making 450–550 nm visible lasers the standard for underwater wireless optical communication.

3. Quantum Limits: Energy per Photon (E = hν)

If higher frequencies yield higher bandwidth, why not move straight to ultraviolet or X-rays for maximum data rates?

The limiting factor is energy quantization. According to Planck's relation (E = hν), higher-frequency photons carry significantly higher individual energies. As you move up into high-energy ultraviolet or X-ray frequencies, communication enters a photon-starved regime. Generating and detecting these high-energy photons requires immense power, and individual photons introduce high quantum shot noise and hardware degradation.

Conclusion

Building high-throughput, secure communication architecture requires balancing bandwidth density, medium attenuation, and quantum energy limits. Infrared lasers dominate long-haul fiber backbones, radio dominates wide-area coverage, and blue-green optical links rule subsea domains.
